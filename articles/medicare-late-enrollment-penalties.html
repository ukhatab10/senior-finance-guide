Medicare Late Enrollment Penalties: How They Work and How to Avoid Them

By the Senior Finance Guide team · Updated November 2026 · Reading time: about 8 minutes

If you sign up for Medicare late, you may pay a penalty added to your premium — every month, for as long as you have that coverage. The penalties are permanent, and they grow with each year or month you wait. Here is how they work, what they cost in 2026, and how to avoid them.

Key Facts at a Glance

  • Part B penalty: 10% of the standard Part B premium for each full year you delay. In 2026, that is $20.29 a month per year late — permanent.
  • Part D penalty: 1% of the national base beneficiary premium for each full month without drug coverage. Fourteen uncovered months costs $5.50 a month in 2026 — permanent.
  • Part D has a 63-day rule: no penalty unless a single break in drug coverage lasted 63 days or more.
  • Job-based health coverage from current employment protects you from the Part B penalty. COBRA and retiree coverage do not.
  • Extra Help removes the Part D penalty, and enrolling in a Medicare Savings Program generally removes the Part B penalty.

Why Medicare Has Penalties

Medicare works best when healthy people sign up at 65, not only when they get sick. The penalties exist to discourage waiting. If you could wait until you needed care and then join at the same price, the program would cost far more for everyone.

That is why both main penalties are permanent. They are not one-time fines. The extra amount is added to your monthly premium for as long as you have that part of Medicare, and it is recalculated each year as premiums change.

The good news: the penalties only apply if you delay without a good reason. If you have qualifying coverage from a current job, you can wait safely. The sections below explain exactly what counts.

The Part B Penalty

Part B covers doctor visits and outpatient care. Its standard premium in 2026 is $202.90 a month. The late enrollment penalty is 10% of the standard premium for each full 12-month period you could have had Part B but did not sign up.

Two details matter. First, only full 12-month periods count. If you are 11 months late, you owe nothing. If you are 12 months late, you owe 10%. If you are 35 months late, you owe 20% — because only two full 12-month periods have passed.

Second, the penalty is recalculated every year. It is always 10% of the current standard premium per year late, so as the premium rises, so does your penalty.

Here is what the Part B penalty costs at the 2026 premium of $202.90 a month:

Two years late means an extra $40.58 a month, or about $487 a year, for life. Five years late means an extra $101.45 a month. That is why signing up on time matters so much.

Worth knowing

Your Initial Enrollment Period is 7 months long: the 3 months before the month you turn 65, your birthday month, and the 3 months after. Sign up in the first 3 months and your coverage starts the month you turn 65.

The Part D Penalty

Part D is Medicare’s prescription drug coverage. Its penalty works by the month, not by the year. You owe 1% of the national base beneficiary premium for each full month you went without Part D or other creditable drug coverage. “Creditable” means coverage that is at least as good as standard Part D — for example, drug coverage from a current employer or from the Veterans Administration.

The national base beneficiary premium is $38.99 a month in 2026 and $41.33 in 2027. The penalty is rounded to the nearest $0.10 and recalculated each year as the base premium changes.

Months without drug coveragePenaltyExtra cost per month in 2026

12 months12%$4.70

The same 14 months of missed coverage would cost $5.80 a month in 2027, because the base premium rises to $41.33. The penalty looks small at first, but it is permanent and it grows every year.

There is also a grace rule: the 63-day rule. You owe no Part D penalty unless a single continuous break in creditable drug coverage lasted 63 days or more after your Initial Enrollment Period ended. Short gaps between plans do not trigger it.

The Part A Penalty

Part A covers hospital care, and most people get it premium-free because they or their spouse paid Medicare taxes for at least 10 years. If you do not qualify for premium-free Part A and you delay signing up, there is a penalty: 10% added to the Part A premium. Unlike the others, it is not permanent — it lasts twice as long as you waited. If you waited 2 years, you pay the extra 10% for 4 years. This penalty is rare, because most people have premium-free Part A.

How to Avoid the Penalties

The most common safe reason to delay is job-based coverage. If you or your spouse is still working and you have group health coverage based on that current employment, you can delay Part B without a penalty. When the job or the coverage ends, you get an 8-month Special Enrollment Period to sign up for Part B penalty-free.

Be careful about what does not count. COBRA — the coverage you can keep paying for after leaving a job — does not protect you from the Part B penalty. Neither does retiree coverage from a former employer. Many people learn this too late: they keep COBRA or retiree coverage past 65, skip Part B, and then face a permanent penalty. If you are on COBRA or retiree coverage, sign up for Part B during your Initial Enrollment Period.

For Part D, the key is to keep creditable drug coverage at all times, or enroll in a Part D plan when you first become eligible. Even a low-cost Part D plan protects you from the penalty later.

Two programs can erase penalties if you qualify. Extra Help — the Part D Low-Income Subsidy — removes the Part D penalty and resets your uncovered months to zero while you have it. Enrolling in a Medicare Savings Program generally removes the Part B penalty. Both programs are for people with limited income; your state Medicaid agency and the Social Security Administration can tell you if you qualify.

If You Already Missed Your Window

If you missed your Initial Enrollment Period, you can still sign up during the General Enrollment Period, January 1 to March 31 each year. You will owe the penalty if you delayed without qualifying coverage, but signing up sooner stops the penalty from growing. Every extra year of delay adds another 10% to the Part B penalty, and every extra month adds another 1% to the Part D penalty.

If you believe a penalty was applied in error — for example, because a federal employee gave you wrong information — you can ask for reconsideration. The Social Security Administration decides Part B penalty cases, and your drug plan, working with CMS, decides Part D cases.

What to Do Next

If you are approaching 65, mark your Initial Enrollment Period on a calendar: it starts 3 months before your birthday month. If you are still working, ask your employer’s benefits office whether your coverage is creditable for Part D and whether it lets you delay Part B penalty-free. Get the answer in writing.

For free, unbiased help, contact your state’s SHIP office — the State Health Insurance Assistance Program. SHIP counselors do not sell insurance. You can also call 1-800-MEDICARE (1-800-633-4227) or the Social Security Administration at 1-800-772-1213.

Common Questions

Does the penalty ever go away?

Not on its own. The Part B and Part D penalties are permanent and last as long as you have that coverage. The only exceptions are programs like Extra Help and Medicare Savings Programs, which can remove the penalty while you qualify.

I am still working at 66. Do I have to sign up for Part B?

Not if you have group health coverage based on your current employment or your spouse’s current employment. That coverage lets you delay Part B penalty-free. When it ends, you get an 8-month Special Enrollment Period to sign up.

Does COBRA count as coverage that lets me delay Part B?

No. COBRA and retiree coverage do not protect you from the Part B late enrollment penalty. If you rely on COBRA or retiree coverage, sign up for Part B during your Initial Enrollment Period.

How is the Part D penalty figured if I had drug coverage from my job?

Months when you had creditable drug coverage do not count against you. Only full months without Part D or other creditable coverage add to the penalty, and only if a single break lasted 63 days or more.

Can I appeal a penalty I think is wrong?

Yes. You can ask for reconsideration. The Social Security Administration decides Part B penalty cases. For Part D, your drug plan decides, working with the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services.
