Social Security COLA: The 2026 Increase and What’s Coming in 2027

By the Senior Finance Guide team · Updated November 2026 · Reading time: about 8 minutes

Social Security checks went up 2.8% in 2026, and a bigger raise — 3.6% — is coming in 2027. These yearly raises are called cost-of-living adjustments, or COLAs. Here is how they are set, what they mean in dollars, and why the raise in your check may be smaller than the headline number.

Key Facts at a Glance

  • The 2026 COLA is 2.8%. The average retired worker’s benefit rose by $56 a month, to about $2,071.
  • The 2027 COLA is 3.6%, announced October 14, 2026. The average retired worker’s benefit rises by about $75 a month — the largest COLA since the 8.7% paid in January 2023.
  • The COLA is automatic. It is based on inflation measured by the CPI-W, comparing July–September prices with the same months of the last COLA year.
  • A COLA can never be negative. If prices fall, your benefit stays the same — it is never cut.
  • The Part B premium is taken from most Social Security checks, so the 2026 premium increase of $17.90 a month eats part of the COLA raise.

What a COLA Is and How It Is Set

COLA stands for cost-of-living adjustment. Each year, the Social Security Administration (SSA) raises benefits by a percentage tied to inflation, so that rising prices do not eat away your buying power. You do not have to apply for it. It happens automatically.

The percentage comes from the CPI-W — the Consumer Price Index for Urban Wage Earners and Clerical Workers — published by the Bureau of Labor Statistics. SSA compares the average CPI-W for July, August, and September of the current year with the average for those same months in the last year a COLA was paid. If prices rose, benefits rise by the same percentage.

Two protections are built in. The COLA can never be negative: if prices fall, your benefit is not cut. And it can be zero in a year with no inflation — your check simply stays the same.

For the 2026 COLA, the third-quarter CPI-W average was 317.265, compared with 308.729 in the third quarter of 2024. That increase of about 2.8% became the 2026 COLA.

The 2026 COLA: 2.8%

The 2026 COLA of 2.8% was announced on October 24, 2025 — about a week later than usual, because a government shutdown delayed the inflation data. It applies to about 71 million Social Security beneficiaries and about 7.5 million SSI recipients — roughly 75 million Americans in total. SSI is Supplemental Security Income, the program for people with limited income and resources who are aged, blind, or disabled.

Two other 2026 figures changed alongside the COLA. The maximum amount of earnings subject to Social Security tax — the taxable earnings maximum — is $184,500 in 2026. And one Social Security credit, the building block of eligibility, costs $1,890 in earnings in 2026.

For context, the 2025 COLA was 2.5%, and the 2.8% figure for 2026 is close to the 50-year median. The recent record is the 8.7% COLA paid in January 2023, when inflation was at its highest in decades.

Worth knowing

Your COLA percentage is the same as everyone else’s, but your dollar raise depends on your benefit. A 2.8% raise on a $2,000 check is $56. On a $1,000 check, it is $28. The bigger your benefit, the bigger your raise in dollars.

The 2027 COLA: 3.6%

The 2027 COLA is 3.6%, announced by SSA on October 14, 2026. It is the largest COLA since the 8.7% paid in January 2023, and it reaches about 76 million Americans: nearly 72 million Social Security beneficiaries and about 7.3 million SSI recipients.

The higher Social Security benefits begin with benefits payable in January 2027. The increased SSI payments begin a little earlier, on December 31, 2026.

The earnings-test limits matter if you claim Social Security before full retirement age and keep working. In 2027, you can earn up to $25,560 a year without any benefits being withheld — up from $24,480 in 2026. In the year you reach full retirement age, the limit rises to $67,920. Once you pass full retirement age, there is no earnings limit at all.

What the Raise Really Means for Your Check

The COLA is a gross raise — the amount before deductions. For most people, the Medicare Part B premium is taken straight out of the Social Security check, so the check you actually receive reflects the premium too.

In 2026, the Part B premium rose by $17.90 a month, from $185.00 to $202.90. For the average retired worker, that means the $56 COLA raise was partly offset by the $17.90 premium increase — leaving a net gain of about $38.10 a month. Your own numbers will differ: the premium increase is the same for everyone on the standard premium, but your COLA raise depends on the size of your benefit.

One protection softens this: a rule called “hold harmless” limits Part B premium increases for many people already collecting Social Security, so that their check does not go down. The rule is complex and does not apply to everyone, but in general terms it means a premium increase cannot make your Social Security check smaller than it was.

As of this writing, the 2027 Part B premium has not been announced, so it is too early to say how much of the 2027 COLA raise will be offset by the premium. Watch for the announcement from CMS, usually in the fall.

How to Check Your New Amount

SSA sends each beneficiary a personalized COLA notice in December, showing your old benefit amount, your new amount, and your deductions. The notice also appears online in late November in the Message Center of your my Social Security account at ssa.gov. That notice is the best place to see your exact new amount, because it reflects your own benefit and your own deductions.

If you do not have a my Social Security account, you can create one at ssa.gov. It also lets you check your earnings record, estimate future benefits, and manage direct deposit.

What to Do Next

There is nothing you need to do to receive the COLA — it is automatic. But the new year is a good time to check three things. First, read your December COLA notice and confirm the new amount matches what lands in your bank account. Second, if you are still working and claiming benefits before full retirement age, note the higher 2027 earnings-test limits. Third, remember that a bigger benefit can mean a bigger tax bill: Social Security benefits are taxable for many people, and the COLA raises the amount that counts.

If you have questions about your benefit, call SSA at 1-800-772-1213 or visit ssa.gov.

Common Questions

Do I have to apply for the COLA?

No. The cost-of-living adjustment is automatic. SSA applies it to your benefit each January, and you will see the new amount in your January payment.

Can my benefit go down if prices fall?

No. The COLA can never be negative. If the CPI-W falls, your benefit stays the same. It can be zero in a year with no inflation, but it will never cut your check.

Why is my raise smaller than the COLA percentage suggests?

The most common reason is the Part B premium, which is deducted from most Social Security checks. In 2026, the premium rose $17.90 a month, which took part of the $56 average COLA raise. Your notice from SSA shows your exact new amount after deductions.

When will I see the 2027 COLA in my check?

The higher Social Security benefits begin with benefits payable in January 2027. Increased SSI payments begin December 31, 2026.

Does the COLA apply to SSI too?

Yes. The same COLA applies to Supplemental Security Income. About 7.5 million SSI recipients received the 2026 increase, and about 7.3 million will receive the 2027 increase.
