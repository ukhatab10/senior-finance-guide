Social Security: Claim at 62 or Wait Until 70?

By the Senior Finance Guide team · Updated November 2026 · Reading time: about 8 minutes

You can start Social Security as early as age 62, or wait until age 70 and get a much larger monthly check. Claiming early cuts your benefit by up to 30 percent for life. Waiting adds 8 percent a year. This article walks through the numbers in plain English.

Key Facts at a Glance

  • Full retirement age is 67 for anyone born in 1960 or later.
  • Claiming at 62 with a full retirement age of 67 means a permanent 30% cut to your monthly benefit.
  • Waiting from 67 to 70 adds 8% a year, for a total boost of 24%.
  • The maximum monthly benefit in 2026 is $2,969 at 62, $4,207 at full retirement age, and $5,181 at 70.
  • The average retired worker gets about $2,071 a month in 2026.

What Is Full Retirement Age?

Full retirement age is the age at which you get 100 percent of your Social Security benefit. Social Security calls that full amount your primary insurance amount, or PIA for short. Your PIA is based on your highest-earning 35 years of work.

Your full retirement age depends on the year you were born. If you were born in 1960 or later, it is 67. If you were born between 1943 and 1954, it is 66. If you were born between 1955 and 1959, it falls between 66 and 67, adding two months for each birth year.

You can claim as early as age 62. But you must have earned 40 Social Security credits to qualify for retirement benefits. That works out to roughly 10 years of work. In 2026, you earn one credit for every $1,890 in covered earnings, and you can earn up to four credits a year.

What Happens If You Claim at 62?

Claiming early means a smaller check every month, for the rest of your life. The cut is permanent. It does not reset to the full amount when you reach full retirement age.

Here is how the cut works if your full retirement age is 67. Claiming at 62 means claiming 60 months early. Social Security reduces your benefit by 5/9 of 1 percent for each of the first 36 months early, a 20 percent cut. Then it reduces your benefit by 5/12 of 1 percent for each additional month, adding another 10 percent. The total reduction is 30 percent.

The Social Security Administration gives a simple example. If your full benefit would be $1,000 a month, claiming at 62 brings it down to $700 a month. That $300 a month is gone for good.

Annual cost-of-living adjustments, called COLAs, still apply to your benefit. But they apply to the reduced amount, not the full amount. So the gap between claiming early and claiming on time never closes.

Worth knowing

The average benefit for people who claim at age 62 is $1,424.40 a month, according to the Social Security Administration. Compare that with the overall average of about $2,071 a month for retired workers in 2026. The early-claim cut shows up clearly in real checks.

What Happens If You Wait Until 70?

Waiting past full retirement age works in the opposite direction. For every year you delay, up to age 70, your benefit grows by 8 percent. That works out to 2/3 of 1 percent for each month you wait. These increases are called delayed retirement credits.

If your full retirement age is 67 and you wait until 70, that is three years of delayed credits. Your benefit grows by 24 percent, to 124 percent of your full amount. On a $1,000 full benefit, that is $1,240 a month instead of $1,000.

The credits are simple, not compounded, and each year's 8 percent is figured on your full benefit amount, which already includes cost-of-living adjustments. The credits stop at age 70, so there is no reason to wait longer.

Claiming at 62, 67, or 70: Side by Side

The table below shows two views of the same choice: the Social Security Administration's $1,000 example, and the maximum monthly benefit in 2026, which applies to someone who earned at or above the taxable earnings cap for 35 years.

Claiming ageBenefit on a $1,000 full amountMaximum benefit in 2026
62 (earliest)$700 a month$2,969 a month
67 (full retirement age)$1,000 a month$4,207 a month
70 (latest worth waiting for)$1,240 a month$5,181 a month

Look at the gap between the first and last rows. On the $1,000 example, the difference between claiming at 62 and claiming at 70 is $540 a month. That is $6,480 a year, every year, for the rest of your life. The trade-off is that you give up eight years of checks while you wait.

What If You Keep Working?

Many people claim early and keep working. If you do that before full retirement age, Social Security applies an earnings test. In 2026, you can earn up to $24,480 a year without any effect on your benefits. If you earn more than that, Social Security withholds $1 in benefits for every $2 you earn above the limit.

The rules are more generous in the year you reach full retirement age. In that year, the limit is $65,160, and only $1 is withheld for every $3 you earn above it. Once you pass full retirement age, there is no earnings limit at all. You can earn as much as you like and keep every dollar of your benefit.

Withheld benefits are not lost. When you reach full retirement age, Social Security recalculates your benefit and raises your monthly amount to give you credit for the months your benefits were withheld. The earnings test delays money; it does not take it away.

What About Your Spouse?

First, spousal benefits. A spouse can claim a benefit based on your work record, worth up to 50 percent of your full benefit amount. But that 50 percent only applies if your spouse claims at their own full retirement age. If your spouse claims at 62, the spousal benefit is reduced by up to 35 percent (for a full retirement age of 67).

Second, survivor benefits. When one spouse dies, the surviving spouse can receive a benefit based on the deceased worker's benefit. Because of that, delaying your own claim can raise the amount your surviving spouse would receive later. If you are the higher earner in the household, this is one of the strongest reasons to consider waiting.

The Break-Even Question

People often ask: at what age does waiting pay off? That is, when does the total you collect by waiting pass the total you would have collected by claiming early?

There is no single exact answer, because it depends on your benefit amount and how long you live. As a rough illustration, the total dollars from claiming at 62 versus claiming at 70 generally cross over somewhere in the late 70s to early 80s. If you live well past that point, waiting comes out ahead in total dollars. If you do not reach that point, claiming early comes out ahead.

But total dollars are not the only thing that matters. A larger check also means more income in your oldest years and a larger survivor benefit for your spouse. Think about the monthly income you will need at 85 or 90, not just the total you might collect.

Who Might Claim Early, and Who Might Wait?

There is no one right answer. The best choice depends on your health, your savings, and your plans. Here are the factors people usually weigh.

Claiming early may make sense if you need the income now, if you are in poor health, or if you have no way to bridge the gap until 70.

Waiting may make sense if you are in good health, if you can cover your expenses from savings or work for a few more years, or if you are the higher earner and want to leave a larger survivor benefit for your spouse.

There is also a middle path. You do not have to choose 62 or 70. You can claim at any age in between. Claiming at 65 or 66, for example, means a smaller reduction than claiming at 62, and you still get several years of checks that waiting until 70 would give up.

Worth knowing

Your benefit estimate is personal. It depends on your own earnings history, not on averages. Before you decide, look up your own numbers. They may look very different from the examples in this article.

How to Check Your Own Numbers

The Social Security Administration offers a free online account called my Social Security at ssa.gov. Once you set one up, you can see your estimated benefit at 62, at full retirement age, and at 70, all based on your actual earnings record. You can also check that your earnings record is correct. Errors in that record can lower your benefit, so it is worth a look.

If you prefer to talk to someone, call the Social Security Administration at 1-800-772-1213 or visit your local office. Either way, get your own estimates first. This is one of the biggest financial choices of retirement, and it is mostly permanent.

Common Questions

Can I change my mind after I claim?

There is a narrow window. Within 12 months of starting benefits, you can withdraw your application, but you must repay all the benefits you received, and you can only do this once in your lifetime. After that, the decision is generally final. This is why it pays to decide carefully before you file.

Does claiming early affect my Medicare?

No. Medicare eligibility starts at age 65 regardless of when you claim Social Security. The two programs have separate timelines. If you claim Social Security before 65, you will still need to sign up for Medicare when you turn 65.

Is there any reason to wait past 70?

No. Delayed retirement credits stop at age 70. Your benefit will not grow any further, so waiting longer only means giving up checks for nothing.

What if my spouse earned much less than I did?

Your spouse may qualify for a spousal benefit based on your record, worth up to 50 percent of your full benefit amount if claimed at full retirement age. Delaying your claim also raises the survivor benefit your spouse would receive if you die first.
