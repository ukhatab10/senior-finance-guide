The Medicare Part B Giveback Benefit: What It Is and How Much

By the Senior Finance Guide team · Updated November 2026 · Reading time: about 8 minutes

You may have seen ads promising money back on your Medicare Part B premium. That promise is real, but it is not a government check. It is a feature of some Medicare Advantage plans, called the Part B giveback. Here is how it works, how much it can be worth, and what to check before you switch plans for it.

Key Facts at a Glance

  • The official name is the Medicare Part B Premium Reduction. Most people call it the "giveback."
  • It is a feature of some Medicare Advantage plans, not a separate government program.
  • The highest giveback offered in 2026 is $174.70 a month ($2,096.40 a year).
  • The average giveback is about $75 a month (about $900 a year).
  • The giveback can never be more than the standard Part B premium: $202.90 a month in 2026.
  • You do not qualify if Medicaid or a Medicare Savings Program already pays your Part B premium.

What the Giveback Is, and What It Is Not

Let us start with the basics. Medicare Part B covers doctor visits, outpatient care, and other medical services. In 2026, the standard Part B premium is $202.90 a month, or $2,434.80 a year. For most people, that premium is deducted straight from their Social Security check.

The giveback is a feature that many Medicare Advantage plans offer. Medicare Advantage, also called Part C, is an alternative to Original Medicare offered by private insurance companies. A plan with a giveback pays part of your Part B premium for you. That is the whole idea. The plan covers some of your premium, so less comes out of your check.

What the giveback is not: it is not a separate government check, and it is not a rebate program you can sign up for on its own. You only get it by joining a Medicare Advantage plan that offers it. And the money does not arrive as cash in your hand. It shows up as a smaller deduction from your Social Security check, or as a smaller bill if you pay your Part B premium directly to Medicare.

Worth knowing

Television and online ads often show the biggest giveback amount available anywhere. Your amount depends on the plans offered in your county. It may be much smaller than the number in the ad. Always check what is actually available where you live.

How Much Can You Get Back?

Giveback amounts vary widely by plan and by county. Here are the 2026 figures.

Giveback amountPer monthPer year
Highest offered in 2026$174.70$2,096.40
AverageAbout $75About $900
Lowest amountsAs little as $10As little as $120

There is a hard ceiling. The giveback cannot be more than the standard Part B premium itself, which is $202.90 a month in 2026. So even the most generous plan cannot give you back more than you pay in premiums.

To see what this means in practice, take the average giveback of about $75 a month. Your Part B premium is $202.90. With the giveback, the deduction from your Social Security check drops to about $127.90 a month. Over a year, that is about $900 back in your pocket. With the highest giveback of $174.70, the deduction drops to $28.20 a month.

How the Money Reaches You

The process is automatic once you are enrolled in a giveback plan, but it is not instant. It can take a few months for the giveback to show up after you enroll. Do not be surprised if your first one or two checks after switching still show the full Part B deduction.

If your Part B premium is deducted from your Social Security check, the giveback reduces that deduction. If you pay your Part B premium directly, for example by quarterly bill, the giveback reduces the amount you are billed. Either way, you keep paying your Part B premium. The plan simply pays part of it on your behalf.

One important detail: the giveback applies only to the standard Part B premium. It does not reduce IRMAA surcharges. IRMAA stands for Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount. It is an extra charge added to the Part B premium for people with higher incomes. In 2026, the standard premium applies to incomes up to $109,000 for a single person or $218,000 for a married couple filing jointly. Above that, surcharges apply, rising to as much as $689.90 a month at the top. If you pay IRMAA, the giveback does not lower that extra charge.

Who Qualifies, and Who Does Not

To get a giveback, you must meet three conditions. First, you must be enrolled in Medicare Parts A and B. Second, you must pay your own Part B premium. Third, you must live in an area where a Medicare Advantage plan with a giveback is offered. Giveback plans are not available everywhere. Availability depends on your county.

You do not qualify if Medicaid or a Medicare Savings Program already pays your Part B premium. Those programs help people with limited income pay Medicare costs. If one of them is already covering your premium, there is nothing left for a giveback plan to pay, so you cannot get the giveback on top of it.

If you are not sure whether you qualify for a Medicare Savings Program, it is worth checking. These programs can pay your Part B premium in full, which is worth more than most givebacks. Our related article on Medicare Savings Programs explains the income limits and how to apply through your state Medicaid agency.

The Trade-Off: Read the Whole Plan

Here is the part the ads leave out. Plans with the biggest givebacks sometimes have higher copays, smaller doctor networks, or higher deductibles. A copay is the flat amount you pay for a visit or a prescription. A network is the list of doctors and hospitals your plan works with. A deductible is the amount you pay yourself before the plan starts paying.

That means a plan offering $174.70 a month back could still cost you more over the year than a plan with a smaller giveback, or no giveback at all, if its copays and deductibles are higher or if your doctors are out of network. Out-of-network care usually costs more, and some plans cover very little of it.

The right way to compare plans is by total yearly cost, not by the giveback number alone. Add up the premium, the expected copays for the care you actually use, the deductible, and the cost of your prescriptions. Then subtract the giveback. That total is the number that matters. A big giveback on a plan that does not fit your needs is not a bargain.

How to Find a Giveback Plan in Your Area

The best place to look is the Medicare Plan Finder at medicare.gov/plan-compare. Enter your ZIP code, and it will show you the Medicare Advantage plans available in your area, including whether they offer a giveback and how much. You can also enter your medicines to see estimated drug costs under each plan.

You can switch into a giveback plan during the Annual Enrollment Period, which runs from October 15 to December 7 each year. Changes take effect on January 1. If you are already in a Medicare Advantage plan, you also have the Medicare Advantage Open Enrollment Period from January 1 to March 31, during which you can switch to a different Medicare Advantage plan once, or return to Original Medicare.

If you want help comparing plans, talk to a SHIP counselor. SHIP stands for State Health Insurance Assistance Program. It offers free, unbiased Medicare counseling in every state. There is no sales pitch and no cost. You can also call 1-800-MEDICARE (1-800-633-4227).

A Quick Checklist Before You Switch

Before you join a plan for its giveback, run through this list.

First, check that your doctors and your hospital are in the plan's network. Second, check that your prescriptions are on the plan's formulary, which is the list of drugs the plan covers. Third, look at the plan's copays and deductible, and estimate your total yearly cost. Fourth, confirm the exact giveback amount for your county, not the amount in an advertisement. Fifth, remember that it can take a few months for the giveback to show up in your check.

If the plan passes all five checks, the giveback is real money back in your pocket every month. If it fails one of them, the giveback may not be worth the switch.

Common Questions

Is the giveback a government payment?

No. It is a feature of some Medicare Advantage plans. The plan pays part of your Part B premium for you. The government does not send you a check.

Can I get the giveback with Original Medicare?

No. The giveback is only available through Medicare Advantage plans. If you have Original Medicare, with or without a Medigap policy, there is no giveback.

How long does it take for the giveback to start?

It can take a few months after you enroll before the smaller deduction shows up in your Social Security check or your Medicare bill. The timing varies, so do not worry if it does not appear right away.

Does the giveback lower my IRMAA surcharge?

No. The giveback only reduces the standard Part B premium. If you pay an income-related surcharge on top of the standard premium, the giveback does not reduce that surcharge.

Can I lose the giveback?

Yes, in a few ways. If you leave the Medicare Advantage plan, the giveback stops. If the plan changes its benefits for the next year, the giveback amount can change. Plans can change their benefits each year, so check your plan's Annual Notice of Change every fall.
