The $6,000 Senior Tax Deduction (2025–2028): Who Qualifies

By the Senior Finance Guide team · Updated November 2026 · Reading time: about 8 minutes

A new federal tax law gives most Americans age 65 and older a deduction of up to $6,000 a year for tax years 2025 through 2028. A married couple where both spouses qualify can claim up to $12,000. The deduction comes on top of the standard deduction, and even people who itemize can claim it. This article explains who qualifies, how the deduction shrinks at higher incomes, and how to claim it on your tax return.

Key Facts at a Glance

  • The deduction is up to $6,000 per person age 65 or older by December 31 of the tax year. A qualifying married couple can claim up to $12,000.
  • It applies to tax years 2025, 2026, 2027, and 2028. It ends after 2028 unless Congress extends it.
  • It is in addition to the standard deduction and the extra standard-deduction amount for age 65+. People who itemize can claim it too.
  • It phases out above $75,000 of modified adjusted gross income for single filers and $150,000 for joint filers, and disappears entirely at $175,000 single and $250,000 joint.
  • It is NOT a repeal of tax on Social Security benefits. Benefits are taxed the same way as before; the deduction only lowers your overall taxable income.

What Is the $6,000 Senior Tax Deduction?

The deduction was created by the 2025 federal tax law, Public Law 119-21. The Internal Revenue Service calls it the "enhanced deduction for seniors." It is written into the tax code at section 151(d)(5)(C).

A tax deduction lowers the amount of your income that is taxed. It is not a check from the government, and it is not a credit that comes straight off your tax bill. Instead, it reduces your taxable income, which in turn reduces the tax you owe. A $6,000 deduction means $6,000 less of your income is subject to federal income tax.

The deduction is available for four tax years: 2025, 2026, 2027, and 2028. After 2028 it ends, unless Congress passes a law to extend it. The $6,000 amount and the income thresholds are fixed in the law. They do not rise with inflation, so the deduction is worth the same dollar amount in each of the four years.

Who Qualifies?

The main requirement is age. You must be 65 or older by December 31 of the tax year. For the 2026 tax year, that means you were born before January 2, 1962. If you turn 65 on January 1, 2027, you do not qualify for 2026, because you were not 65 by the end of that year.

Each qualifying person gets their own deduction of up to $6,000. A married couple where both spouses are 65 or older by December 31 can claim up to $12,000 between them. If only one spouse is 65 or older, only that spouse's $6,000 applies.

There are two other requirements. You must have a valid Social Security number listed on your tax return. And if you are married, you must file a joint return with your spouse to claim the deduction. Married couples who file separately cannot claim it.

Worth knowing

This deduction stacks on top of what you already get. It is in addition to the standard deduction, and in addition to the extra standard-deduction amount that taxpayers age 65 and older already receive. And unlike many deductions, it is available whether you take the standard deduction or itemize your deductions. You do not have to choose one or the other.

How the Phase-Out Works

Not everyone gets the full $6,000. The deduction shrinks as your income rises, and it disappears entirely above a certain level. The income measure used is modified adjusted gross income, usually shortened to MAGI. For most people, MAGI here is simply the adjusted gross income (AGI) shown on Form 1040, your main federal tax return form.

The phase-out works like this. If your MAGI is at or below $75,000 as a single filer, or $150,000 on a joint return, you get the full $6,000. Above those thresholds, your deduction is reduced by 6 percent of every dollar of MAGI over the threshold. The deduction reaches zero at $175,000 for single filers and $250,000 for joint filers.

On a joint return, the 6 percent reduction applies separately to each spouse's $6,000. That means each spouse's deduction is reduced by the same dollar amount, and each can fall to zero on its own. A couple is not treated as having one combined $12,000 deduction that phases out once.

Single filer MAGIDeduction
$75,000 or less$6,000
$100,000$4,500
$125,000$3,000
$150,000$1,500
$175,000 or more$0

Here is how the arithmetic works for a single filer with MAGI of $100,000. That is $25,000 above the $75,000 threshold. Six percent of $25,000 is $1,500. Subtract $1,500 from $6,000 and you get a $4,500 deduction. At $125,000, the excess is $50,000, six percent is $3,000, and the deduction is $3,000. At $175,000, the excess is $100,000, six percent is the full $6,000, and nothing is left.

A Worked Example for a Couple

Consider a married couple, both age 68, filing jointly with a MAGI of $160,000 for 2026. The joint threshold is $150,000, so their MAGI is $10,000 above it. Six percent of $10,000 is $600.

Because the reduction applies separately to each spouse, each spouse's $6,000 deduction is reduced by $600, leaving $5,400 each. Together, the couple claims $10,800. If their MAGI had been $150,000 or less, they would have claimed the full $12,000. If it had reached $250,000, both deductions would be gone.

Note that the phase-out is gradual. Going a little over the threshold costs you only a little of the deduction — 6 cents of deduction for each dollar of income over the threshold. There is no sudden cliff where you lose the whole amount at once.

What This Deduction Is Not

There has been a good deal of confusion about this deduction, so it is worth being clear about what it does not do.

First, it is not a repeal of the tax on Social Security benefits. Your Social Security benefits are taxed exactly the same way as before. The deduction only lowers your overall taxable income. If part of your Social Security benefits were taxable before this law, they are still taxable. The deduction may reduce your total tax bill, but it does not change how benefits themselves are treated.

Second, it does not lower the income figure Medicare uses to set your Part B premium. Medicare charges higher premiums, called IRMAA surcharges, to people with higher incomes, based on your modified adjusted gross income from two years earlier. This deduction does not reduce that figure. Claiming it will not lower your Medicare premiums.

Third, it is not permanent. As the law stands, the deduction is available for tax years 2025 through 2028 only. It ends after 2028 unless Congress extends it. Do not build long-term plans around it lasting beyond that date.

How to Claim It

You claim the deduction on Schedule 1-A, a form that goes with your Form 1040. You enter the amount on Form 1040, line 13b. Schedule 1-A and its instructions walk you through the calculation, including the phase-out if your income is above the threshold.

If you use tax preparation software, it should ask whether you or your spouse were 65 or older by December 31 of the tax year and calculate the deduction for you. If you use a tax preparer, mention the deduction and bring your dates of birth. If you prepare your own return on paper, read the Schedule 1-A instructions carefully, and check the IRS Publication 554, the Tax Guide for Seniors, which covers this deduction along with other tax rules that affect older taxpayers.

Remember the filing requirement: married couples must file jointly to claim this deduction. If you and your spouse usually file separately, you will need to file a joint return for any year in which you want to claim it.

Common Questions

I turn 65 in December 2026. Do I qualify for 2026?

Yes, as long as you are 65 by December 31, 2026. The rule looks at your age on the last day of the tax year, not at the start of it. For 2026, anyone born before January 2, 1962, qualifies.

Can I claim it if I itemize my deductions?

Yes. This deduction is available whether you take the standard deduction or itemize. It is claimed in addition to either one, along with the extra standard-deduction amount for age 65 and older.

My spouse is 63 and I am 67. How much can we claim?

Only your $6,000, subject to the phase-out on your joint income. Your spouse does not qualify until the tax year in which they turn 65 by December 31. In the year your spouse turns 65, you can claim up to $12,000 between you, again subject to the phase-out.

Does the deduction reduce my Social Security tax?

No. Social Security benefits are taxed the same way as before. The deduction lowers your overall taxable income, which can lower your total federal income tax, but it does not change the rules for taxing benefits.

Will the $6,000 amount go up with inflation?

No. The law sets the amount at $6,000 with no inflation adjustment, and the income thresholds are fixed as well. The deduction is the same $6,000 in each of the four years it is available, 2025 through 2028.

Does claiming this deduction affect my Medicare premiums?

No. It does not lower the modified adjusted gross income that Medicare uses to decide whether you pay IRMAA surcharges on your Part B premium. Your Medicare premiums are unaffected by this deduction.
