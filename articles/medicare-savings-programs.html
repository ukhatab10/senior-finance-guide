Medicare Savings Programs: How They Pay Your Part B Premium (QMB, SLMB, QI)

By the Senior Finance Guide team · Updated November 2026 · Reading time: about 8 minutes

In 2026, the standard Part B premium alone is $202.90 a month — that is $2,434.80 a year, taken out of your Social Security check before you ever see it. If your income is limited, four state-run programs called Medicare Savings Programs can pay that premium for you, and one of them pays your deductibles and copays too. This article explains each program, the 2026 income and asset limits, and how to apply.

Key Facts at a Glance

  • Medicare Savings Programs are four state-run programs — QMB, SLMB, QI, and QDWI — that help people with Medicare and limited income pay Medicare costs.
  • QMB, SLMB, and QI all pay your Part B premium ($202.90 a month in 2026). QMB also pays your deductibles, coinsurance, and copayments.
  • 2026 monthly income limits for a single person: QMB $1,350, SLMB $1,616, QI $1,816. A $20 monthly income disregard is applied before your income is compared to the limit.
  • Enrolling in QMB, SLMB, or QI automatically qualifies you for Extra Help, the program that lowers prescription drug costs.
  • Your state Medicaid agency decides who qualifies. Some states use higher limits or have dropped the asset test, so apply even if you think you are slightly over.

What Are Medicare Savings Programs?

Medicare Savings Programs, often shortened to MSPs, are four programs run by the states with federal funding. They exist for one reason: to help people who have Medicare and a limited income pay the costs that Medicare itself does not cover.

Those costs add up fast. Part B, which covers doctor visits and outpatient care, charges a standard premium of $202.90 a month in 2026. You must also meet a $283 annual deductible before Part B pays anything, and after that you pay 20 percent of approved costs as coinsurance. Original Medicare has no annual limit on what you can owe out of pocket.

Depending on which program you qualify for, the state pays your Part B premium — and in the case of QMB, your deductibles, coinsurance, and copayments too. You keep your Medicare coverage exactly as it is; the program simply picks up some or all of the bills Medicare leaves behind.

Your state Medicaid agency runs these programs and decides who qualifies. Medicaid is the joint federal and state health program for people with limited income. You apply through your state Medicaid agency, not through Medicare or Social Security.

Worth knowing

The standard Part B premium of $202.90 a month applies at incomes of $109,000 or less for a single person and $218,000 or less for a married couple. People with higher incomes pay surcharges on top of it. Medicare Savings Programs serve the other end of the income scale — people for whom $202.90 a month is a real burden.

The Four Programs at a Glance

Three of the four programs — QMB, SLMB, and QI — pay your Part B premium. The fourth, QDWI, pays only the Part A premium for a smaller group of people. The income limits below are the 2026 federal limits for the 48 contiguous states and Washington, D.C.; limits are higher in Alaska and Hawaii.

QMB: The Most Help

QMB, short for Qualified Medicare Beneficiary, is the most generous of the four programs. If you qualify, the state pays your Part B premium of $202.90 a month, and your Part A premium too if you have one. Most people get Part A, the hospital part of Medicare, premium-free, but some people who did not work long enough under Social Security must pay for it.

QMB also pays the costs Medicare leaves you with: your $283 annual Part B deductible, your 20 percent coinsurance, and your copayments. It covers the Part A hospital deductible too, which is $1,736 per benefit period in 2026. A benefit period begins the day you enter a hospital and ends after you have been out for 60 days in a row.

QMB has the lowest income limit: $1,350 a month for a single person and $1,824 a month for a couple in 2026. Before your income is compared to the limit, a standard $20 a month is subtracted — an income disregard — so your actual income can be $20 a month above the limit and still qualify.

SLMB: Help With the Part B Premium

SLMB, short for Specified Low-Income Medicare Beneficiary, pays your Part B premium only — not deductibles, coinsurance, or copayments. If your income is too high for QMB but within the SLMB limit, the state still picks up $202.90 a month, saving you $2,434.80 over a full year.

To qualify, you must have both Part A and Part B. The 2026 income limits are $1,616 a month single and $2,184 a month for a couple, after the $20 monthly disregard.

QI: The Same Premium Help, With a Catch

QI, short for Qualifying Individual, works like SLMB: it pays your Part B premium and nothing else. Its income limits are a little higher — $1,816 a month single and $2,455 a month for a couple in 2026, after the $20 disregard. You must have both Part A and Part B.

QI has two features the other programs do not. First, it is first-come, first-served each year: funding is limited, so once the money is used up, later applicants may have to wait until the next year. Second, you must reapply every year — coverage does not carry over.

QDWI: For Working People With a Disability

QDWI, short for Qualified Disabled and Working Individual, is a narrower program. It pays only the Part A premium, for people under 65 who have a disability, are working, and lost their premium-free Part A because their earnings rose above a certain level. Its income limit is much higher than the others — $5,405 a month single and $7,299 a month for a couple in 2026 — but its asset limit is much lower.

Income and Asset Limits for 2026

Qualifying depends on two things: your income and your resources. Resources are assets such as money in a bank account, stocks, and bonds. The limits below are the 2026 federal limits.

Remember the $20 monthly income disregard: your state subtracts $20 from your monthly income before comparing it with the limit. That small adjustment can make the difference if your income is just above a limit.

Apply even if you think you are over the limit

The limits in this article are the federal floors. States may use higher income limits, may not count certain kinds of income or resources, and some have eliminated the asset test entirely. If your income is close to a limit, apply anyway and let your state agency decide. You could gain $2,434.80 a year in premium help.

The Extra Help Bonus

There is one more reason to apply: enrolling in QMB, SLMB, or QI automatically qualifies you for Extra Help, the federal program that lowers prescription drug costs under Part D. No separate application is needed.

Extra Help is worth a great deal on its own — the Social Security Administration estimates about $5,700 a year on average. It pays your Part D premium up to a regional benchmark amount, removes your Part D deductible, and caps copays at no more than $5.10 for a generic drug and $12.65 for a brand-name drug per prescription in 2026. It also removes the Part D late enrollment penalty while you have it.

How to Apply

Apply through your state Medicaid agency. Every state has one, though the name varies — it may be called the Department of Health and Human Services or the Medicaid office. Find yours through Medicare.gov or by calling 1-800-MEDICARE (1-800-633-4227).

Have your Medicare card, proof of income, and information about your bank accounts and other resources ready. Your state agency will tell you exactly which documents it needs. For QI, apply as early in the year as you can, because funding is first-come, first-served.

For free, unbiased help with your application, contact your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP). SHIP counselors are in every state, they do not sell insurance, and their help costs nothing.

Common Questions

Do I lose my Medicare if I enroll in a Medicare Savings Program?

No. You keep your Medicare coverage exactly as it is. A Medicare Savings Program only pays some of the costs Medicare leaves you with, such as the Part B premium. Your doctors and your coverage do not change.

What if my income is a little above the limit?

Apply anyway. The $20 monthly income disregard means your income counts as $20 lower than it really is, and states may use higher limits, may not count certain income, and some have removed the asset test. Only your state Medicaid agency can say whether you qualify.

Does QMB really pay my hospital deductible?

Yes. QMB pays Medicare deductibles, coinsurance, and copayments, including the Part A hospital deductible of $1,736 per benefit period in 2026. SLMB and QI do not pay these costs — they pay the Part B premium only.

I already have Extra Help for my prescriptions. Do I still need a Medicare Savings Program?

They cover different costs. Extra Help lowers your prescription drug costs under Part D. A Medicare Savings Program pays your Part B premium and, in the case of QMB, your deductibles and copays. Having one does not make the other unnecessary — in fact, qualifying for QMB, SLMB, or QI is one way to qualify for Extra Help automatically.
