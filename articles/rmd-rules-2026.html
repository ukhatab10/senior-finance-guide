RMD Rules 2026: Required Withdrawals From 401(k) and IRA

By the Senior Finance Guide team · Updated November 2026 · Reading time: about 8 minutes

Once you reach your 70s, the IRS requires you to start taking money out of most retirement accounts each year. These are called required minimum distributions, or RMDs. Miss one and you face a steep penalty. Here are the rules for 2026, in plain English.

Key Facts at a Glance

  • RMDs start at age 73 if you were born in 1951–1959, and at age 75 if you were born in 1960 or later.
  • Your RMD is your December 31 balance from the prior year divided by your IRS life expectancy factor. At age 73, the factor is 26.5 — about 3.77% of the balance.
  • A $500,000 balance at age 73 means an $18,868 RMD in 2026.
  • The deadline is December 31 each year. Your first RMD may be delayed until April 1 of the following year — but then you take two RMDs in one year.
  • Missing an RMD costs a 25% excise tax on the amount you failed to withdraw, reduced to 10% if you correct it in time.
  • Roth IRAs have no lifetime RMDs for the owner, and since 2024 Roth 401(k)s and Roth 403(b)s do not either.

What an RMD Is

RMD stands for required minimum distribution. It is the smallest amount the IRS says you must withdraw each year from your tax-deferred retirement accounts. “Tax-deferred” means you did not pay income tax on the money when you put it in; the tax was postponed. The RMD rules make sure that tax is eventually paid.

The amount you withdraw is generally taxable income in the year you take it. That is why RMDs matter for your tax bill: a large RMD raises your taxable income for the year. Because Medicare premiums are based on income, a large RMD can also mean a higher Part B premium in a later year.

You can always take out more than the minimum. The RMD is a floor, not a ceiling. What you cannot do is take out less than the minimum — or nothing at all.

When RMDs Start

The starting age changed under a 2022 law called SECURE 2.0. It depends on the year you were born:

Year of birthRMDs start at age
1951–195973
1960 or later75

The old age-72 rule no longer applies. If you were born in 1953, for example, you turn 73 in 2026, so 2026 is your first RMD year. If you were born in 1960 or later, you do not start until age 75.

RMDs are based on your age during the calendar year. Once you reach your starting age, you owe an RMD every year for the rest of your life, as long as money remains in the accounts.

Worth knowing

Your first RMD is the only one you may delay. You can wait until April 1 of the year after you reach your starting age — but then you take two RMDs in that second year, which can push you into a higher tax bracket. Most people are better off taking the first RMD in its own year.

Which Accounts Have RMDs

RMDs apply to tax-deferred accounts — the accounts where your contributions and growth were not taxed when they went in. The main ones are:

457(b)Yes
Roth IRANo — not for the owner, ever
Roth 401(k) / Roth 403(b)No — not since 2024

Roth accounts are different because you already paid tax on the money you put in. That is why Roth IRAs have no lifetime RMDs for the owner. Since 2024, the same is true for Roth 401(k)s and Roth 403(b)s, thanks to SECURE 2.0.

If you inherit an IRA, different rules apply — most non-spouse beneficiaries must empty the account within 10 years. This article covers RMDs from your own accounts only.

How to Figure Your RMD

The formula is simple: take the balance of the account on December 31 of the prior year and divide it by your life expectancy factor from the IRS Uniform Lifetime Table, found in IRS Publication 590-B. Your 2026 RMD uses your December 31, 2025 balance.

At age 73, the factor is 26.5. That means your RMD is about 3.77% of your balance. Here is what that looks like at different balances:

Balance on Dec 31, 2025Factor at age 732026 RMD
$100,00026.5$3,774
$200,00026.5$7,547
$500,00026.5$18,868
$1,000,00026.5$37,736

The factor shrinks as you get older, so the percentage you must withdraw rises each year. Your bank or brokerage usually calculates your RMD for you and will tell you the amount — but the responsibility for taking it is yours.

If you have more than one account of the same type, you figure the RMD for each account separately. You can take the money as one lump sum or spread it through the year, in monthly or quarterly withdrawals. You can also have federal income tax withheld from the distribution, so you are not hit with a big tax bill in April.

The Deadline — and the First-Year Trap

Your RMD must be taken by December 31 of each year. There are no extensions.

The one exception is your very first RMD. You may delay it until April 1 of the following year. But that is usually a trap: if you delay, you must take two RMDs in that second year — the delayed one by April 1 and the current year’s by December 31. Both count as taxable income in the same year, which can push you into a higher tax bracket and raise your Medicare premiums down the road.

Worked example: suppose you turn 73 in 2026 and your first RMD is $18,868. If you take it in 2026, that is your only RMD income for the year. If you delay it to March 2027, you will have $18,868 plus your 2027 RMD as taxable income in 2027. Unless you have a specific reason to delay, take the first RMD in its own year.

What Happens If You Miss One

The penalty for missing an RMD is an excise tax of 25% of the amount you failed to withdraw. SECURE 2.0 cut it from the old 50%, but 25% is still steep: miss an $18,868 RMD and the penalty is $4,717.

The penalty drops to 10% if you correct the mistake within the correction window — generally by the end of the second year after the missed RMD, and before the IRS assesses the penalty. So the same missed $18,868 RMD would cost $1,887 if corrected in time.

If you realize you missed an RMD, take the missed amount as soon as you notice, and report it on IRS Form 5329. Do not wait and hope the IRS does not notice — your account custodian reports your distributions to the IRS, so a missing RMD is easy to spot.

Ways to Handle Your RMD

Spread it out. You do not have to take the whole RMD at once. Many people take monthly or quarterly withdrawals, which also spreads the tax withholding across the year.

Withhold tax. You can have federal income tax withheld from each distribution. This is a simple way to avoid owing a large amount when you file your return.

Give it to charity. If you are 70½ or older, you can make a qualified charitable distribution (QCD): a donation sent directly from your IRA to a charity. The donated amount counts toward your RMD and is excluded from your taxable income. The gift must go directly from the IRA to the charity — it cannot pass through your hands first.

What to Do Next

First, find out whether 2026 is an RMD year for you: if you were born in 1951–1959 and are 73 or older this year, it is. Next, check the December 31, 2025 balance of each of your tax-deferred accounts and confirm the RMD amount with your bank or brokerage. Then set up your withdrawals — lump sum or spread out — with tax withholding, well before December 31.

If your situation is more complex — multiple accounts, an inherited IRA, or a missed RMD — the IRS Publication 590-B explains the rules in detail, and a qualified tax advisor can help you plan. Free tax help for people 60 and older is also available through the IRS Tax Counseling for the Elderly program.

Common Questions

Do I have to take an RMD if I am still working?

Yes, for your IRAs. RMDs from a traditional IRA apply whether or not you are still working. The still-working exception applies only to some employer plans, and only if you do not own 5% or more of the company.

Can I take my RMD from just one account if I have several IRAs?

Yes, for IRAs: you add up the RMDs for all your traditional IRAs and take the total from one or more of them. Employer plans such as 401(k)s are different — each plan’s RMD generally must come from that plan.

Do Roth IRAs really have no RMDs?

Correct — for the owner. A Roth IRA has no lifetime required withdrawals for the person who owns it. Since 2024, Roth 401(k)s and Roth 403(b)s also have no lifetime RMDs. Beneficiaries who inherit a Roth account follow different rules.

What if I take out more than the minimum?

That is fine. The extra amount counts as taxable income in the year you take it, but it does not count toward next year’s RMD. Each year’s RMD must be taken in that year.

I missed an RMD last year. What should I do?

Take the missed amount right away, and report it on IRS Form 5329. If you correct it within the correction window — generally by the end of the second year after the missed RMD — the penalty is 10% instead of 25%.
