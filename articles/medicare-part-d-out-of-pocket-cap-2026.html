Medicare Part D Out-of-Pocket Cap 2026: What Changed

By the Senior Finance Guide team · Updated November 2026 · Reading time: about 8 minutes

Medicare Part D now has a yearly limit on what you pay out of pocket for covered prescription drugs. In 2026, that limit is $2,100. Once your spending reaches it, your covered drugs cost $0 for the rest of the year. Here is what changed, what counts toward the cap, and what to watch for.

Key Facts at a Glance

  • The Part D out-of-pocket cap is $2,100 in 2026. It was $2,000 in 2025.
  • Once you reach the cap, covered Part D drugs cost $0 for the rest of the calendar year.
  • The maximum Part D deductible in 2026 is $615.
  • The cap will rise to $2,400 in 2027. It is indexed and goes up each year.
  • Your monthly plan premium does not count toward the cap.
  • Medicare-covered insulin is capped at $35 a month per covered prescription.

What the Cap Is

Medicare Part D is the part of Medicare that covers prescription drugs. You get it through a private drug plan or through a Medicare Advantage plan that includes drug coverage. Before 2025, there was no limit on what you could pay out of pocket for Part D drugs in a year. People taking expensive medicines could face thousands of dollars in costs.

The Inflation Reduction Act, a federal law passed in 2022, changed that. It created a yearly cap on out-of-pocket spending for covered Part D drugs. The cap started at $2,000 in 2025. For 2026, it is $2,100. For 2027, it will be $2,400. The cap is indexed, which means it is adjusted and rises each year.

Here is the simple version: add up what you pay during the year for covered drugs. When that total reaches $2,100 in 2026, you stop paying. Your covered drugs cost $0 for the rest of the calendar year. The count starts over on January 1.

Worth knowing

Many websites still quote $2,000 as the Part D cap. That was the 2025 figure. For 2026, the correct number is $2,100. If a website says $2,000, it has not been updated.

2025, 2026, and 2027 at a Glance

The table below shows how the main Part D numbers have changed. The deductible is the amount you pay yourself before your plan starts paying its share. Each plan sets its own deductible, but it cannot be higher than the maximum shown here.

The national base beneficiary premium is a figure the government publishes each year. It is used to calculate the Part D late enrollment penalty. In 2026, it is $38.99 a month; in 2027, it will be $41.33 a month. It is not necessarily what you pay for your own plan. Your plan's actual premium can be higher or lower. The Inflation Reduction Act limits how fast this base premium can rise: no more than 6 percent a year from 2024 through 2029.

What Counts Toward the Cap, and What Does Not

Not every dollar you spend on medicines counts toward the $2,100. Here is the breakdown.

What counts: your deductible, your copays, and your coinsurance for covered drugs. A copay is a flat dollar amount you pay for a prescription, such as $10 or $45. Coinsurance is a percentage of the drug's cost, such as 25 percent. A covered drug is one that is on your plan's formulary. A formulary is the list of drugs your plan covers.

What does not count: your monthly plan premium. Drugs that are not on your plan's formulary. And drugs that Medicare excludes from coverage altogether. Those costs are real, but they do not move you closer to the cap.

This matters when you choose a plan. A plan with a low premium may look cheap, but if your medicines are not on its formulary, you pay for them yourself and none of it counts toward the cap. Always check that your drugs are covered before you pick a plan.

A Worked Example

Here is a simple example of how the cap can work in practice. The dollar amounts for the prescriptions are made-up round numbers to show the arithmetic. Your own costs will depend on your plan and your medicines.

Suppose you take a brand-name drug that costs you $300 a month in copays after your deductible. In January, you first pay your plan's $615 deductible. Then you pay $300 a month for the drug. By the end of April, you have paid $615 plus four months at $300, which is $1,815. In May, another $300 payment takes you to $2,115. But you only owe up to the $2,100 cap, so you pay $285 that month instead of $300. From June through December, that covered drug costs you $0.

Your total out-of-pocket drug spending for the year is $2,100, plus your monthly premiums. Without the cap, you would have kept paying $300 a month all year. The cap is what stops the bill from growing.

Most people will not reach the cap. It matters most for people who take expensive brand-name or specialty medicines. But it is there for everyone, and it means no one faces unlimited drug costs in a year.

The Donut Hole Is Gone

Older readers may remember the "donut hole." That was a gap in Part D coverage. After you and your plan had spent a certain amount, you entered a gap where you paid a much larger share of your drug costs, until your spending reached a second threshold. Then coverage kicked back in.

The donut hole was eliminated on January 1, 2025, by the Inflation Reduction Act. It no longer exists. Now there is a single, simple structure: you pay your deductible and your copays or coinsurance, and once your out-of-pocket total hits the cap, your covered drugs cost $0 for the rest of the year.

Insulin: $35 a Month

Medicare-covered insulin is capped at $35 a month for each covered prescription. If you take more than one type of insulin, the $35 cap applies to each covered prescription separately.

What you pay for insulin still counts toward your $2,100 out-of-pocket cap. So insulin costs move you closer to the point where all your covered drugs cost $0.

Spreading Costs Across the Year: The Medicare Prescription Payment Plan

One problem with the cap is timing. Many people pay most of their drug costs early in the year, when the deductible is due and copays pile up. The Medicare Prescription Payment Plan, sometimes called M3P, is designed to help with that.

Here is how it works. Instead of paying large drug costs all at once early in the year, you can spread them across monthly payments for the rest of the year. You still pay the same total amount. The plan does not lower your costs. It just smooths them out so you are not hit with a big bill in January.

The payment plan has been available since 2025. You must opt in through your drug plan. It is free to join, and anyone with a Part D plan can use it. If your drug costs are high early in the year, it is worth asking your plan about it.

What About Premiums and the Late Enrollment Penalty?

The cap covers what you pay at the pharmacy. It does not cover your monthly plan premium. You keep paying your premium all year, even after you reach the cap.

There is also a late enrollment penalty to know about. If you go without Part D or other creditable drug coverage, you pay a penalty of 1 percent of the national base beneficiary premium for each full month you were without coverage. Creditable coverage means drug coverage that is at least as good as standard Part D, such as coverage from an employer or the VA. The penalty is permanent and is added to your premium every month. That is one more reason to sign up for drug coverage when you first become eligible, even if you do not take many medicines yet.

If you have limited income, a program called Extra Help can pay much of your Part D costs, including your premium up to a regional benchmark amount, and it sets your deductible to $0. See our related article on Extra Help for the details.

What to Do During Open Enrollment

Medicare's Annual Enrollment Period runs from October 15 to December 7 each year. During that window, you can join, switch, or drop a Part D plan. Changes take effect on January 1. This is the time to check whether your current plan still covers your medicines at a fair price.

Use the Plan Finder at Medicare.gov to compare plans by your ZIP code and your drug list. It will show you each plan's premium, deductible, and estimated yearly cost for your medicines. You can also get free, unbiased help from a SHIP counselor. SHIP stands for State Health Insurance Assistance Program. Counselors in every state help people with Medicare questions at no cost and with no sales pitch. You can also call 1-800-MEDICARE (1-800-633-4227).

Common Questions

Does the cap apply to drugs I buy without insurance?

No. The cap only covers drugs that are on your Part D plan's formulary. If you pay out of pocket for a drug your plan does not cover, that spending does not count toward the cap.

What happens when I reach the cap in the middle of the year?

Once your out-of-pocket spending on covered drugs reaches $2,100 in 2026, you pay $0 for covered Part D drugs for the rest of the calendar year. You still pay your monthly plan premium. The count resets on January 1.

Is the $2,100 cap the same for every plan?

Yes. The cap is set by law and applies to all Part D plans. What varies by plan is the premium, the deductible (up to the $615 maximum), and which drugs are on the formulary.

Does the cap include my Part B drugs?

No. The cap covers Part D prescription drugs only. Some drugs given in a doctor's office or hospital are covered under Part B instead, and those have different cost rules. Part B covers 80 percent of approved costs after the deductible, and you pay 20 percent coinsurance.

Will the cap keep going up?

Yes. The cap is indexed and rises each year. It was $2,000 in 2025, it is $2,100 in 2026, and it will be $2,400 in 2027.
