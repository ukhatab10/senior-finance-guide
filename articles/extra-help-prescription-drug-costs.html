Extra Help (Low Income Subsidy): How It Caps Your Prescription Drug Costs

By the Senior Finance Guide team · Updated November 2026 · Reading time: about 8 minutes

Prescription drugs are one of the biggest bills in retirement, and a single brand-name medicine can cost hundreds of dollars a month. If your income and savings are limited, a federal program called Extra Help can cut those costs sharply: it pays most of your Part D drug plan premium, removes your deductible entirely, and caps what you pay at the pharmacy counter. This article explains what it covers, who qualifies in 2026, and how to apply.

  • With Extra Help, your Part D deductible is $0, and in 2026 copays are capped at $5.10 for a generic drug and $12.65 for a brand-name drug per prescription.

What Is Extra Help?

Extra Help is the everyday name for a federal program officially called the Part D Low-Income Subsidy, or LIS. Part D is the part of Medicare that covers prescription drugs. You get it through a private drug plan, and like most insurance, it comes with a monthly premium, an annual deductible, and copays each time you fill a prescription.

A premium is what you pay each month to keep your coverage. A deductible is what you pay out of pocket before your plan starts paying. A copay is the fixed amount you pay at the pharmacy for each prescription. Extra Help lowers all three. It is run by the Social Security Administration, and it is free to apply.

The help is substantial: the Social Security Administration estimates Extra Help is worth about $5,700 a year on average. For someone taking several prescriptions a month, that can be the difference between affording medicine and going without it.

What Extra Help Pays For

Extra Help covers three kinds of drug costs. First, it pays your Part D premium up to a regional benchmark amount — a dollar figure set for your region. If your plan's premium is at or below it, Extra Help pays the whole premium; if it costs more, you pay only the difference.

Second, it removes your Part D deductible entirely. In 2026, the maximum Part D deductible is $615 a year — without Extra Help, you would pay that out of pocket before your plan started covering your drugs. With Extra Help, your deductible is $0.

Third, it caps what you pay at the pharmacy: in 2026, no more than $5.10 for a generic drug and no more than $12.65 for a brand-name drug per prescription. A generic drug is a copy of a brand-name drug with the same active ingredient, sold under its chemical name at a lower price. The caps apply to every covered prescription you fill.

The copay caps rise slightly in 2027, to no more than $5.80 for a generic drug and $14.40 for a brand-name drug per prescription. Even at those levels, the caps keep the cost of each prescription low and predictable.

Worth knowing

Extra Help also removes the Part D late enrollment penalty while you have it. That penalty is normally 1 percent of the national base beneficiary premium for each full month you went without drug coverage, and it lasts as long as you have Part D. With Extra Help, you do not pay it.

Everyone Gets the Full Subsidy Now

Extra Help used to come in two levels: a full subsidy and a partial subsidy, with less help for people on the partial tier. That changed on January 1, 2024, under the Inflation Reduction Act. Since that date, everyone who qualifies gets the full subsidy — there is no longer a partial tier.

That same law also created a yearly cap on out-of-pocket drug costs for everyone with Part D. In 2026, once your out-of-pocket spending on covered drugs reaches $2,100, you pay $0 for covered Part D drugs for the rest of the calendar year. With Extra Help, your costs are already so low that you may never come near that cap.

Who Qualifies in 2026

Qualifying depends on your income and your resources — assets such as money in bank accounts, stocks, and bonds. Your home, household goods, and car do not count.

The income limit for 2026 is at or below 150 percent of the federal poverty level: $23,940 a year for a single person ($1,995 a month) and $32,460 a year for a couple ($2,705 a month). The limits are higher in Alaska and Hawaii. For reference, the 2026 federal poverty level for one person is $15,960 a year.

The resource limits are $16,590 for a single person and $33,100 for a couple in 2026. On top of that, the program allows a $1,500-per-person burial-expense allowance, raising the effective limits to $18,090 single and $36,100 for a couple. If your savings are near the limit, that allowance may put you under it.

Automatic Qualification

Many people qualify without ever filling out an application. If you receive Supplemental Security Income (SSI), Medicaid, or help through a Medicare Savings Program — QMB, SLMB, or QI — you qualify automatically. The Social Security Administration and your state already have the information they need.

If you are not sure whether you qualify automatically, ask the Social Security Administration. If you do not qualify automatically, you can still apply on your own, as the next section explains. It costs nothing to apply.

How to Apply

Extra Help is run by the Social Security Administration, so that is where you apply. You can apply online at ssa.gov, call Social Security at 1-800-772-1213, or fill out Form SSA-1020, the paper application, and send it in.

Before you apply, gather your income information and a list of your resources, such as bank account balances. If you want free help, your local State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) can walk you through the application. SHIP counselors are in every state, they do not sell insurance, and their help costs nothing. You can also call 1-800-MEDICARE (1-800-633-4227) with questions about how Extra Help works with your drug plan.

There is no deadline: you can apply at any time of year, and your help begins once your application is approved. If your income or resources change later, tell the Social Security Administration, because your eligibility may change too.

One Important Limitation

Extra Help is not available in Puerto Rico, the U.S. Virgin Islands, Guam, the Northern Mariana Islands, or American Samoa. Those territories have their own programs to help residents with drug costs — contact your local Medicaid office to ask what is available where you live.

A Worked Example

Imagine a retired woman living alone. Her only income is Social Security, $1,900 a month, and she has $8,000 in savings. She takes three prescription medicines: two generics and one brand-name drug.

Her income of $1,900 a month is below the 2026 single-person limit of $1,995 a month, and her savings of $8,000 are well below the $16,590 resource limit. She qualifies. Her Part D deductible drops to $0, her plan premium is paid up to the benchmark, and each month she pays no more than $5.10 for each generic and $12.65 for the brand-name drug — $22.85 a month at most for all three. Without Extra Help, she could have faced a $615 deductible first and much higher copays after that.

Common Questions

Do I have to reapply for Extra Help every year?

Not in most cases. The Social Security Administration reviews eligibility on an ongoing basis and will contact you if it needs updated information. If your income or resources change, report the change. This is different from the QI Medicare Savings Program, which requires a new application every year.

Does Extra Help pay for drugs my plan does not cover?

No. Extra Help lowers the cost of drugs covered by your Part D plan. If a drug is not on your plan's formulary — the list of drugs the plan covers — Extra Help does not pay for it. If your doctor prescribes one, ask your plan about an exception, or talk to a SHIP counselor about switching plans.

What if my plan's premium is higher than the benchmark?

You pay only the difference between your plan's premium and the benchmark amount. If your plan costs much more than the benchmark, compare plans during the Annual Enrollment Period, October 15 to December 7, when you can switch to a plan whose premium is fully covered.

I get Medicaid. Do I need to apply?

No. If you receive Medicaid, Supplemental Security Income (SSI), or help through a Medicare Savings Program (QMB, SLMB, or QI), you qualify for Extra Help automatically. You do not need to file a separate application.

Does Extra Help cover insulin?

Yes. Medicare-covered insulin is capped at $35 per month per covered prescription for everyone with Part D, with or without Extra Help. With Extra Help, your insulin copay is capped even lower, at the brand-name limit of $12.65 per prescription in 2026.
